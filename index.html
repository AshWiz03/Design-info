One in five parents has opted their child out — and schools don't know what to do

The right to refuse data sharing is being used more and more. But the reading support, the read-aloud function and the adapted tasks all sit inside the same system.

Text: Hanna Bergström · Published 9 October 2036

Background · What LYRA is

LYRA is the AI learning companion used in 214 of Sweden's 290 municipalities. It follows a pupil from year to year and from school to school, adapts tasks as they work, reads text aloud, and sends guardians a weekly engagement score.

In the classroom it appears as a layer in the teacher's glasses: every pupil carries a name, a focus value and a suggestion for what to do next. All of it runs on data collected about the child.

Which is why a pupil whose guardians have opted out shows up with no ring at all, marked “No data · Basic mode”.

When Ingrid Falk puts on her glasses at 08:10, twenty-two of her pupils light up with a name, a focus score and a suggestion from LYRA. Two of them do not.

“They're just there. No tag, no support, no suggestions. The system politely tells me to plan their support myself,” says Ingrid Falk, special needs teacher at a school in Västerort.

Since the School Data Act came into force in 2031, guardians have the right to opt their child out of learning data sharing. More and more do. But read-aloud, adapted tasks and the AI companion's support all need data to work. A pupil who opts out of the data also loses the help.

Not every refusal is about privacy Accessibility & inclusionEthics & values

The Authority records that a guardian has opted out, not why. When Kollegiet asked 140 of those households directly, data protection came first — but by a smaller margin than the debate assumes. Close to a third gave a different answer altogether: the format did not suit the child.

“We had no problem with them knowing what he reads,” says Hanna Lindevall, a parent in Sundsvall. “He simply could not work like that. A task that changes every forty seconds, a voice in his ear, something marking his attention all day. He came home empty. He is not against school. He is against being accompanied.”

The distinction matters for schools, because the two groups want opposite things. A family refusing on privacy grounds would take every support function back tomorrow if it ran on the school’s own machines. A family refusing the format wants less of the system in the room, not more of it under better rules.

Neither is offered. SD-14 has one box.

What the layer costs, and who decides

Lyra Learning and one competitor now cover 214 of Sweden's 290 municipalities between them. Switching supplier means a pupil's earlier years do not come with them, which school heads describe as the reason they stay.

The classroom glasses are supported for three school years. After that they stop receiving updates and must be replaced. Four of the five municipalities Kollegiet contacted call the cost “recurring and impossible to plan for”. Two have cut their teaching-material budget to pay for it.

LYRA Basic and LYRA 4 — what a municipality gets

Basic, covered by the national agreement: lesson delivery, attendance, a weekly score for guardians.

LYRA 4, paid separately: read-aloud, adapted tasks, classroom layer, suggestions to the teacher, support flags.

A pupil without data sharing receives Basic regardless of what the municipality pays for.

For guardians, the weekly score arrives on Friday afternoon. The School Data Authority's own survey this spring found that guardians open the LYRA Home app on average forty times a week, with the heaviest use on Saturdays and Sundays. “The worry has moved into the weekend, and on Monday it is in the teacher's inbox,” says Petra Ödman, researcher in educational measurement.

Kollegiet's survey of 600 special needs teachers shows that pupils without data sharing wait on average five weeks longer for adapted material. Many teachers say they make it by hand in the evenings.

Lyra Learning, whose system is used in 214 of Sweden's 290 municipalities, rejects the criticism. “LYRA Basic gives every pupil a good education. The support functions need data to work. That is how the technology works, not a choice we made,” says press officer Jonas Ek.

Parents who opt out describe a process that pulls in one direction. Opting out needs a paper form, signatures from both guardians and up to six weeks of handling. Opting back in takes one tap in the LYRA Home app.

A new version of the opt-out form applies from 1 January. The School Data Authority says it “clarifies which functions stop”.

Ask the union lawyer Sustainability & wellbeing

“Do I have to explain the weekly score after working hours?”

Every Friday at 16:00 the engagement scores go out to guardians. By 16:20 my phone starts buzzing. Last week a father wanted to know why his son dropped from 78 to 72, and asked me to go through the minute-by-minute data with him on Saturday. Do I have to?Teacher, year 5, Västerort

Short answer: no. Your working hours are set by the central agreement and your school's local agreement. Contact with guardians outside those hours is not part of your duties unless your employer has decided so and compensates you for it.

Note also that the engagement score is produced by the system, not by you. You have no obligation to interpret it. Questions about how the score is calculated should go to your school's data controller.

A tip from members: turn off notifications from LYRA Home after 17:00, and tell guardians at the start of term when you can be reached.

“I said no. Now my son waits for help that used to come automatically”

Leila Haddad, parent and nurse, Göteborg

Every Friday at four, my phone told me how engaged my nine-year-old had been that week. A number between 0 and 100. One week it asked if he was sleeping well, because he had looked away from the board.

I work in healthcare. I know what it means to collect data about a body. So in August we filled in the form, both of us signed, and we posted it.

What nobody told us was that the read-aloud function would go with it. My son has dyslexia. The help he had for two years was switched off the day the form was processed. His teacher now prints texts in large font in the evenings. She shouldn't have to.

Consent that costs your child their support is not consent. It's a price.

I am not against technology. I am against a system where saying no is a paper form and saying yes is one tap. Where privacy is something you can afford only if your child doesn't need help.

Support and data collection must be separated. Read-aloud does not need to know where my son looks.

“They ask me what their number was before they ask what they got wrong”

Annika Ferm, school counsellor, Västerort

A nine-year-old sat in my room in September and asked whether 71 was a bad person. Not a bad week. A bad person. I have had some version of that conversation eleven times this term.

Children do not read the score the way the guidance notes assume. They do not see an estimate based on gaze, voice and task data. They see a weekly verdict about them, sent to their parents, produced by something they cannot see working and cannot argue with.

What I notice most is the middle of the week. From about Wednesday the older ones begin managing the number instead of the work — sitting still, facing the board, keeping their hands where they can be seen. They have worked out what the system counts.

They have not become more engaged. They have become better at looking engaged.

The younger ones take it differently. For them the number is simply true, the way the weather is true. A seven-year-old cannot hold the idea that a measurement might be wrong about them, and nobody has given them the words for it.

I am not asking for the system to be switched off. I am asking for two things that cost nothing. Let the child see their own score before their guardians do. And stop sending it on a Friday at four, when every adult who could explain it has gone home.
